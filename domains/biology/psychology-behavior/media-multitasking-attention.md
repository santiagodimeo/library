# Media multitasking and attention

The media-multitasking hypothesis holds that people who habitually consume several media streams at once develop worse attentional control. It is cited as settled far more often than the evidence supports.

Wiradhany — an author of the meta-analysis usually cited for the effect — subsequently ran a study of 261 participants and found nothing. Media multitasking score did not predict more mind-wandering (χ²(1) = 1.41, p = .236), did not predict greater vulnerability to external distractors (χ²(3) = 1.19, p = .754, with BF₀₁ = 3698.41 favouring the model without the interaction), and did not predict worse overall performance (p = .121) (Wiradhany et al. 2020). The same paper reports that in the earlier meta-analysis, only ten of 39 tests of the external-distraction hypothesis were significant and the pooled effect was "weak (Cohen's d = .17)".

What does have experimental support is changing the environment rather than diagnosing the person. Blocking mobile internet for two weeks in a randomized trial of 467 participants improved sustained attention (dz = 0.24, p = .008), mental health (dz = 0.57) and well-being (dz = 0.46), with 90.7% improving on at least one outcome (Castelo et al. 2025).

## Tradeoffs

The intervention result carries its own warning about self-imposed limits: only 119 participants, "25.5% of those who committed", kept the block active for the preregistered 10 of 14 days (Castelo et al. 2025). Three in four motivated adults could not sustain a restriction they chose. The authors also flag demand characteristics and a highly motivated sample.

Note the asymmetry in what each design licenses. The correlational literature asks whether multitaskers *are* different and largely finds they are not; the trial asks whether removing the affordance *helps* and finds it does. Both can be true, and only the second is actionable. See [[deterministic-mediation-of-agents]] for why the external block outperforms the resolution.
